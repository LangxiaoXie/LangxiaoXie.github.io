Part II of Jacques Derrida’s De la grammatologie has become canonical for its sustained reading of Rousseau’s Essay on the Origin of Languages as a paradigmatic instance of phonocentrism, wherein the ontological privilege of the signiﬁed over the signiﬁer—of presence over representation—is asserted without ambiguity. Derrida’s reading identiﬁes in Rousseau a metaphysical commitment to a logocentric system, one that presupposes the transparency of meaning to speech and the originary status of vocal expression in relation to writing. This gesture inaugurates not merely a critique of Rousseau as a metaphysician, but a broader intervention into the limits of interpretation per se , which Derrida performs through a meticulous practice of deconstructive reading. By staging the internal contradictions and aporias within Rousseau’s corpus—oscillating, for instance, between the valorization of nature and the necessity of its supplementation—Derrida renders impossible any stable hermeneutic recovery of Rousseau as a coherent authorial subject. Yet more fundamentally, Derrida’s project situates Rousseau as a synecdoche for the Western metaphysical tradition that, despite Nietzsche’s invocation of its end, continues to reproduce itself through binaries it cannot dissolve.

However, the subsumption of Rousseau under the metaphysical tradition risks effacing a distinct register of his thought—namely, the politico-theoretical and proto- scientiﬁc dimension wherein the primacy lies not in the truth-value of concepts, but in their pragmatic function: the regulation of desire, the formation of civic subjectivity, the utility of pedagogical and discursive formations. Even if Rousseau’s conceptual oppositions— nature/writing , understanding/sensibility , instinct/sociality —are unstable and ultimately deconstructible, they remain structurally indispensable to the thematic architecture of his account of human development. To reject them outright would be to overlook the operational logic by which Rousseau constructs a model of subjectivation and social organization. It is in this spirit that the present essay adopts Derrida’s own ﬁgure of the supplement as its organizing motif: not to simply reinscribe Rousseau within the metaphysics of presence, but to trace how Rousseau’s political and pedagogical thought mobilizes supplementarity as both a structural necessity and an object of suspicion. The supplement—at once that which adds to and that which replaces—will serve here as the conceptual hinge through which the constitutive paradoxes of nature, desire, and sociality are examined.

Accordingly, the problematic reframed through the question of the supplement —that which the human constructs in response to the originary lack left by nature’s withdrawal—offers, this essay contends, a fertile analytic through which to rearticulate Rousseau’s conception, crisis, and wager concerning the human condition.

This condition encompasses a tripartite ﬁguration: man as the natural-transcendental subject of Reveries of the Solitary Walker , men as the socio-plural actors of The Social

Contract , and Man as the historicized and technically constituted being whose

formation is staged in Émile . Given the scope and constraints of this inquiry, the analysis will center on Émile , wherein Rousseau constructs not merely an educational treatise, but a speculative anthropology: a model of individual formation that bears within it a latent politico-psychological doublet. It is in this text, this essay argues, that Rousseau imagines human existence as historically sedimented yet embedded within the cyclical rhythms of nature.

To delineate the speciﬁc contours of the human condition that Rousseau problematizes, the essay proceeds both topically and sequentially, following the structure of Émile from Book II through Book V. Each section interrogates a distinct psycho-political modality of the human supplement to nature. The ﬁrst section engages primarily with Book II , foregrounding the psychoanalytic concept of introjection: the internalization of the Other’s authority in the constitution of the ego and the duplication of consciousness as a concomitant of socialization. The analysis traces Jean-Jacques’s pedagogical deferral of intersubjective encounter as a strategic withholding of symbolic inscription—an effort to produce a subject minimally marked by the superegoic trace. In so doing, Rousseau seeks to preserve a space of non- performative freedom, attenuating the extent to which liberty must be enacted before the gaze of the Other. This formation responds implicitly to the problem outlined in the Discourse on Inequality : how to cultivate virtuous citizens within a corrupted social order, a question Rousseau later reframes in the paradox of the lawgiver—the one who must found law without already presupposing the moral disposition it seeks to engender.

Building upon the argumentation of the ﬁrst section, the second part turns to Émile Book III , wherein the child—now past the stage of pure sensory experience— begins to exercise the faculty of judgment. Crucially, this faculty does not emerge through discursive rationality in the Kantian sense, but through what might be called schematic or imaginative mediation. That is, judgment arises not through abstract reasoning but through an affectively charged relation to appearances—a capacity to synthesize impressions according to a sensibility already valorized. Rousseau’s moral anthropology here suggests that the basis of free will and ethical discernment is not understanding per se , but a more primordial capacity to judge what is good through what is felt as beautiful, or even pleasurable. This aesthetic-moral equivalence, in turn, situates moral judgment at the intersection of two registers: on the one hand, the political-ethical axis of autonomy and free action; on the other, the psychoanalytic economy of enjoyment ( jouissance ), wherein virtue is not merely a normative ideal but a libidinal alignment. What undergirds Rousseau’s vision, therefore, is not simply the moralization of nature, but a fragile harmonization between the subject’s internal affective organization and the external symbolic and material environment in which that subject must act.

The third section then turns to the gendered dyad of Émile and Sophie, introduced in Book V , and examines how this pairing retroactively structures the pedagogical logic of the text. Their differentiation corresponds allegorically to theoretical binaries—such as teleological versus aesthetic judgment—where the former concerns the determination of moral purpose and meaning, while the latter relates to beauty, affect, and the sublime. Within this distribution, woman is assigned a speciﬁc ecological function in the symbolic division of labor: not as a direct agent of political action or moral autonomy, but as a second-order constitutive force. Sophie is not the one who acts, but the one who curates the ﬁeld of action by shaping the conditions under which the male subject's agency can be realized. In Rousseau’s schema, woman functions as the mediator of mediation: she constitutes he who constitutes. This recursive structure implies a paradoxical reciprocity—each gender superior in relation to what the other lacks—yet the balance is asymmetrical. Woman’s role is deﬁned by relationality, by her capacity to serve as the supplement through which man’s freedom and universality can appear as natural. She is, in short, the condition of the possibility of man’s ethical plenitude, precisely through her structural exclusion from it.

Anti-Oedipus

Rousseau, from the outset of Book II , demonstrates an acute awareness of the performative structure of desire—namely, that to desire is already to desire as the Other, to enjoy what one believes the Other would have one enjoy. Enjoyment, in this sense, is not immanent but staged: a performance whose sincerity is irrelevant, since the structure of enjoyment presupposes its mediation by the gaze of an Other.

Rousseau writes that “when children begin to speak, they cry less… one language is substituted for the other,” and notes further that “whatever injury a child may do to himself, it is very rare that he cries when he is alone, unless he hopes to be heard” (77).

Even the expression of pain becomes intelligible only through an assumed intersubjective circuit. When Rousseau observes that “if he sees me keep my composure, he will soon regain his and will believe the injury cured when he no longer feels it,” he points toward the mimetic dimension of affect, wherein the child’s internal state is shaped retroactively by the anticipated response of the adult Other. Crying and speaking thus operate as homologous signifying acts, both emerging as modes of articulating lack, and both contingent on the presumption of a listening, responsive presence. To articulate a need is already to invoke the symbolic Other capable of satisfying it. Desire, in this formulation, is never simply given but is always shaped by the presumption of its legibility and addressability within a shared symbolic space.

It is this dynamic—of desire as produced through the imagined response of the Other—that will later be radicalized through the emergence of the faculté imaginative , which displaces immediate needs into complex representations of absence and possibility.

Yet Rousseau simultaneously posits a distinction between natural need and socially mediated desire , suggesting that certain needs—akin to instincts—are ontologically prior to and structurally independent of the symbolic order. He warns that “one should distrust what they desire but are unable to do for themselves and others have to do for them. Then true need, natural need, must be carefully distinguished from the need… [that] comes only from the superabundance of life of which I have spoken” (86). This differentiation articulates a broader Rousseauian metaphysics in which Nature is ﬁgured as a pure, originary domain—uncorrupted by the alienating mediation of the social—and thus capable of grounding a non- performative mode of existence. Rousseau’s pedagogical imperative, in this respect, is a project of deferral and puriﬁcation: to shield the child from premature entanglement in the web of symbolic desire and instead orient him toward needs that arise from an immanent, self-sustaining vitality. This logic underwrites his recommendation that the child encounter resistance solely in things, never in wills (66)—a principle that seeks to eliminate the dialectical struggle for recognition that would otherwise inaugurate the subject’s entry into the intersubjective ﬁeld. The opposition between resistance as material and resistance as will recurs throughout the text as an ethical and pedagogical antinomy. At this juncture, Rousseau is not yet fully synthesizing the two regimes of desire, but is instead attempting to hold them apart: the one authentic, grounded in biological immediacy; the other performative, a product of mimetic excess. The child must be cultivated in such a way as to preserve the former while resisting the contamination of the latter, even if the viability of such a separation remains theoretically unstable.

In his seminar The Psychoses , Lacan characterizes schizophrenia as a structure marked by the foreclosure— forclusion —of the Name-of-the-Father, the primordial signiﬁer that guarantees symbolic consistency. In the absence of this anchoring function, the symbolic order collapses into a proliferating ﬁeld of undifferentiated signiﬁers, a multiplication of partial "others" without reference to the constitutive Other. One of the deﬁning features of psychosis, then, is the subject’s inability to submit to the transcendental authority required for linguistic signiﬁcation to operate.

Language presupposes a prior act of submission: to speak is already to assume the rule of symbolic law, to accept that signiﬁers are not self-generated but conferred by a network of differential positions instituted through the Other's authorization. This inaugural act—the installation of a stable signifying chain—marks the emergence of the subject as such. It is not that the human being possesses some pre-social, purely natural set of needs; rather, the entry into language implies that desire is always already mediated, inscribed within a social ﬁeld. The psyche, in this account, is irreducibly marked by the trace of the Other—it is constituted through lack, through a structural openness to symbolic inscription, even if this opening can later be disavowed, foreclosed, or perverted in pathological structures.

Psychosis, insofar as it forecloses the introjection of the Other and the attendant structuration of subjectivity via the symbolic order, paradoxically opens a space of resistance to normative processes of social integration. The psychotic structure resists the paternal metaphor—that is, the symbolic substitution that institutes law, language, and desire—thereby suspending the very coordinates by which meaning is stabilized intersubjectively. In this respect, Émile , in the early stages of his formation, can be read as exhibiting a quasi-psychotic relation to the social: his exposure to symbolic mediation is minimal, if not altogether absent. The absence of a differentiated symbolic matrix—of the dialectic of recognition, of intersubjective address—renders the subject vulnerable to the collapse of meaning, which in clinical structures manifests as delusion or hallucination. However, Émile is not strictly unsocialized. He is constantly accompanied by Jean-Jacques, a pedagogical ﬁgure whose presence is paradoxically constitutive and concealed. Rather than being “natural” in any naïve sense, Émile is a subject of a rigorously curated sociality—a sociality mediated not by the multiplicity of others but by a singular, invisible authority that simulates the neutrality of nature. Rousseau’s educator adopts the position of an absent presence, orchestrating the conditions of desire while disavowing any overt imposition of law.

Desire, in this schema, is not repressed but modulated; its emergence is managed through a pedagogy that seeks to bypass the alienation of traditional socialization by installing a substitute Other who pretends not to be one, by which the social is neither fully disavowed nor assumed, but mimetically staged by Jean-Jacques under the sign of nature.

The structural consequences of psychosis—particularly those associated with schizophrenic disorganization—can be discerned within the pedagogical architecture of Émile . Schizophrenia, classically deﬁned by a breakdown in symbolic anchoring, entails a diminished capacity for contextual differentiation and a fundamental opacity regarding the operation of metalanguage. The schizophrenic subject struggles to parse enunciation from statement, seriousness from irony, and thus fails to locate utterances within a shared intersubjective frame. Strikingly, Rousseau’s description of Émile at the conclusion of Book II resonates with this phenomenological condition. When Rousseau observes that “whether he is busy or playing, it is all the same to him. His games are his business, and he is aware of no difference” (161), he is identifying a subject for whom contextual distinctions—between labor and leisure, action and play—have not yet been symbolically instituted. This indeterminacy is not pathological in Émile’s case, but pedagogically intentional: it reﬂects the deliberate suspension of intersubjective mediation. Because Émile has been shielded from socialization—speciﬁcally, from the gaze of the Other—he has no internalized locus of surveillance or symbolic law. He does not “know” what it means to be watched, and hence cannot reﬂexively differentiate between performative registers. The absence of an internalized spectator—a superegoic or symbolic instance—renders all of his activity homologous. Yet Rousseau suggests that it is precisely this unmarked freedom from performative duplicity that will later enable Émile to “command without wishing to do so,” as others will spontaneously “feel his superiority” (162).

What appears as a lack of contextual discernment, akin to schizophrenic disorientation, is reframed as a pre-symbolic unity—one that paradoxically preﬁgures sovereign singularity within the social order.
