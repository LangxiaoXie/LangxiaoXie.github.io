Structure and Genesis of Anti-Oedipus

A communist society would be a society where you… everyone would be allowed to dwell in his or her own stupidity. You know who gave me this idea? Even Fredric Jameson who said, what if you imagine communism not as a perfectly normal society, but like a crazy society that you find like in some Bruegel’s proverbs…You know a madman here; there’s a man who thinks he is a chicken and aaagaga walks like this; there’s a man who thinks he’s Napoleon…You know all this madman politely exists wouldn’t it be nice?

Slavoj Zizek

Unlike A Thousand Plateaus , in which the writing itself enacts a praxis and experimentation of forms that performatively instantiate desiring-production, Anti-Oedipus , the first volume of Capitalism and

Schizophrenia , still presents a discernible central thesis and a functionally—rather than merely

differentially—articulated division into four parts. Yet, as a text marked by the aftermath of May ’68, it is less a neutral delineation or representation of the real than an apparatus that produces, modulates, and subjects desire. If Deleuze and Guattari tirelessly fashion themselves as anti-Hegelian, their writing nonetheless sketches a formal affinity with the dialectic, sustained by repeated, tacit affirmations of its structural logic. Marx, Hegel’s most famous inheritor, is “Hegelian” precisely in the gesture by which he criticizes the idealist residue while sublating the dialectic into a materialist register. In a 1983 interview, Deleuze and Guattari identify themselves, without hesitation, as Marxists. To what extent, then, is their rendering of Marx unabashedly purged of dialectics—without simultaneously evacuating the metaphysical density that analytic Marxism à la Cohen seeks to strip away—perhaps by way of supplementation from what they themselves would call “minor” philosophers such as Spinoza and Bergson? Or are they, in Anti-Oedipus and in something like the sense in which Marx remains Hegelian, still secretly dialecticians?

The essay affords nothing less than a detailed exegesis of Anti-Oedipus , and accounts for the structual strategics of the whole texts, with the presentation of ontological, phenomenological and historiological thematics in each of the first three parts, and explicates from within hidden dialectics Deleuze and Guattari attempt to disregard. In Why Must A Dialectician Learn to Count to Four? , Slavoj Zizek exposits the evolution of dialectical movements as first the passing of being into its own inner opposite, becoming namely abstract negativity, and then, in account for this one-sided determination, negates again the negation so as to pass into self-relating negativity, a universality that is concrete. Zizek emphasizes, however, that in this final negation, the agent of the movement, that is, the mediator, vanishes and presents retroactively from the later moments as if it was never there to begin with. It is argued, therefore, that dialectics must passes into its fourth moments, retracting the vanishing mediator qua the motor of the movement to, again, so to speak, tarry with the negative.

The ontological presentation of machines (paranoid, miraculated, cellibate), together with three syntheses (connective, disjunctive, conjunctive) corresponded to them, can be recognized as the first moment of pure being, or the metaphysical underpinning only to be later developed into its own negation, i.e., the misuses or abuses of the three machines/syntheses as delineated in Part II of the book: the global and specific use of connective synthesis (this and that, instead of open-ended); exclusive, restrictive use of disjunctive synthesis (either/or, instead of A or B or C or…); and finally, segregative, biunivocal use of conjunctive synthesis (this is us not them, instead of nomadically identifying with everything)(110), to which we will come back later. The abstract negation is presented here in Part II only to be resurfaced in Part III, where its logico-historical development is packaged so as to gloss over its moments of genesis and thereby be presented as a concrete or self-relating negation.

It is only here at the end of Part III, Oedipus, which has already be rendered the negation of the real use of the syntheses (116), morphs into standing at the end of a universal history the emancipation of which requires Oedipus’s self-critique (175), and only from where Deleuze and Guattari can begin explicating the positive tasks of schizoanalysis, which is the fourth moment, and wherefore are presented only in the Part IV of the book, “Introduction to Schizoanalysis”.

Speculative Identity Despite elements of the first part are organized into triads––like the (connective, disjunctive, conjunctive) syntheses and the (paranoic, miraculated, celibate) machines, but also that which prefaces the historical moments (territorial, despotic, capitalist) in Part III with their corresponding symptomatology (hysteria, paranoia, schizophrenia) in Part II––the heading of Part I is nevertheless simply the desiring machine (33). A logically ensued question would be, then, why was Part I simply named in relation to the first moment of the syntheses, namely desiring-production, as opposed to body without organ in the second moment or the subject in the third moment? Is it simply structually the most important in the triad or does it somehow encompasses their totality? From a series of equivalences listed in this first part of the book, such as nature = production (3), man = nature (4), production = product (7), nature = history (25), the answer can be speculated by, I would argue, positing the speculative identity among the three moments. Such identity are presented in the first glance, implicitly or not, throughout the text, such as the effacement of the distinction between individual and group fantasy, “[t]he libido internalizes it [the Oedipus] in the form of a castrating duality…[b]ut this duality is artificial, derived, and supposes a direct relationship proceeding from the statement to the collective agents of enunciation in the group fantasy (64),” or when they argue for the co-originarity of the symptoms at the end of Part II, “[t]here are not two groups, there is no difference in nature between neuroses and psychoses. For in any case desiring-production is the cause… (127),” or the identity of psychic and social repression, “[it] is in one and the same movement that the repressive social production is replaced by the repressing family, and that the latter offers a displaced image of desiring- production that represents the repressed as incestuous familial drives (119).” These are all to say, for Deleuze and Guattari, that the difference between those differentiated terms are simply different of régime (31); in both of the economies of each case above, it is “ the same syntheses that are at issue (116),” whence the reason for the subject to desire its own repression.

We have to slow down here, for claiming the identity between psychic and social production, or between neurosis and psychosis are different from claiming the identity of the threefold syntheses that produce them. Even as we have seen from above for Deleuze and Guattari there is an identity of product and production which they presumably took from Spinoza’s metaphysics, let us be more careful and dwell more on the mechanism of the machines. At the beginning of the book Deleuze and Guattari explicates the meaning of the equation product = production, namely “producing is always something ‘grafted onto’ the product; and for that reason desiring-production is production of production, just as every machine is a machine connectd to another machine (6).” Or “The schizophrenic is the universal producer. There is no need to distinguish here between producing and its product. We need merely note that the pure ‘thisness’ of the object produced is carried over into a new act of producing,” and this producing/product identity “constitues a third term in the linear series: an enormous undifferentiated object (7),” which is simply later the body without organ. Deleuze and Guattari subsequently define machine as “a system of interruption or breaks ( coupures ) (36).” “Every machine,” they go on to explain, functions as a break in the flow in relation to the machine to which it is connected, but at the same time is also a flow itself, or the production of a flow, in relation to the machine connected to it. This is the law of the production of production. That is why, at the limit point of all the transverse or transfinite connections, the partial object and the continuous flux, the interruption and the connection, fuse into one : everywhere there are breaks-flows out of which desire weels up, thereby constituting its productivity and continually graftin the process of production onto the product (37).

Hence the self-referential definition of machine: a machine is a machine only when it breaks other machines. But is there a ‘primordial’ machine to begin with? No, since nature is production. What is there at the origin is not a machine to be broken but the break itself . Earlier when the body without organ was defined, too, they relates the “genesis of machine” with “the opposition of the process of production of the desiring-machines” and “the nonproductive stasis of the body without organs” which defines the paranoiac machine, which, however, is merely “an avatar of the desiring-machines (9).” It is clear from here that the genesis of machine can be framed in one of the two ways: first, simply desiring-machine, which by definition breaks other mathines; second, interaction between desiring-production and body without organ, as we showed above. Thus the first instance of desiring- production and the second instance of body without organ, which correspond to connective and disjunctive synthesis, is also, in some ways, a difference of régime. More clearly Deleuze and Guattari have explained this in the lexicon of Marxian categories as they corresponds the three syntheses to production, circulation (recording) and consumption, and converge them under a single term, namely “process,” Conforming to the meaning of the word “process,” recording falls back on ( se rabat sur ) production, but the production of recording itself is produced by the production of production. Similarly, recording is followed by consumption, but the production of consumption is produced in and through the production of recording. This is because something on the order of a subject can be discrned on the recording surface…(16) Deleuze and Guattari attributes three meanings to the word “process,” which correspond respectively to “incorporating recording and consumption with production itself,” “man and nature are…one and the same essential reality, the producer-product,” and “the process should work to a completion (5).” This speculative identity strictly correlates to the one Marx stretched out in the introduction to Grundrisse , …production produces consumption: (1) by creating the material for consumption; (2) by determining the mode of consumption; (3) by creating in the consumer a need for the products which it first posits as objects. It therefore produces the object of consumption, the mode of consumption and the urge to consume. Similarly, consumption produces the predisposition of the producer by soliciting him as a purpose-determining need. The identity of consumption and production thus appears three-fold…(30) This also marks a break from Marx’s eariler humanist philosophy wherein a human nature to be restored from alienation is stipulated. But here, with the identity of production and consumption (and circulation and exchange), we see human to be not different under such metaphysical framework, when he claims, for instance, that “[c]onsumption is directly also production…it is obvious that man produces his own body, e.g., through nutrition, a form of consumption…(28)” Going back to why this was brought up in the first place, this threefold identity between production and (each of) circulation, exchange and consumption in the Grundrisse referenced by Deleuze and Guattari to theorize their three moments of syntheses is evidently borrowed from the threefold identity formulated famously by Hegel, as the three moments of logic. Just to flash it out briefly, in Science of Logic , for instance, Hegel distinguishes the abstract standpoint of the understanding from the dialectical and speculative activity of reason. In the “Preface” to the First Edition he writes that “the understanding ( Verstand ) determines, and holds the determination fixed. Reason ( Vernunft ) is negative and dialectical…; it is positive, since it generates the universal and comprehends the particular therein” (21.8). Applied to the category of identity in the “Doctrine of Essence”, this yields a threefold structure. First, Hegel criticizes the “system of identity” for which “being is only being, nothing only nothing,” a view he calls “abstract identity” (21.18–19). Second, in the chapter on the “essentialities of reflection” identity proves to be “self-contradictory self-relation,” passing into contradiction (11.263 ff.). Finally, this contradiction is aufgehoben in ground, defined as “essence as positive self-identity which, however, at the same time refers itself to itself as negativity” (11.279 ff.). In this sense speculative identity is the “identity of identity and non-identity” Hegel attributes to the absolute at the very beginning of the Logic (21.61).

This threefold of abstract, dialectical, and speculative identity correspond to Marx’s threefold identity of production and consumption, which he explains as an identity in object (each is directly the other), in need (each mediates as the means of the other), and in mode (each creates the other) (30).

We have gotten too far…too far into the bush of Hegelianism. We demonstrated how the threefold syntheses for Deleuze are in fact moments of the same process characterizing production, and how, in Marx, these three moments are moments of identity between production and consumption (and circulation and exchange) excavated from Hegel’s threefold identity of being and thought. The mathematical language of flow implicating transmission, code that directs such transmition, and stocks that accumulates from transmition, corresponds strictly with these threefold syntheses. If that is the case, we now see flow, code and stocks are aspects of the same nature, and it is only realized in capitalism and in the discussion of which Deleuze and Guattari regeared their language to that of the flux , characterized by the transition from a surplus value of code to that of flux. It is here the hierarchy of the three elements disappear and the code, which is supposed to code flows, itself can be come “ flows of code ,” (232) and be coded and overcoded, ad infinitum . But to examine this coalescence among flow, code and stocks, alternatively designated as the merging or the immanentization of production and anti-production, we first need to delineate the genesis in a genealogical fasion, namely the inversely proportioned development of the identity in nature and difference in regime over territorial, despotic and capitalist formation, before the positive tasks of schizoanalysis can be derived, and succeedingly the fourth formation it speculatively brings about.

Protestantism, Jacobinism… Marx’s philosophy of history, as its name indicates, that is, historical materialism, that its rational kernal is as well taken from Hegel. What in Marx were described as the change of relation of production that characterizes land, instruments, and means of consumption which sequentially alienated from the laborer were appropriated in Deleuze and Guattari as the territorial, despotic and capitalist formation.

Again in Zizek’s essay, the three stages was corresponded with the moments of Hegelian triad, only with a minimal difference, that Zizek points out the non-dialecticizable excess of the triad, namely the vanishing mediator and subscribes it historically to the Jacobian essence of protestantism that tries to save the world from feudalism. Therefore this section will first presents the historical formation in succession, together with its evolutionary logic in terms of writing and semiotics, before reinscribing it into dialectics following Zizek’s formultion, and finally searching for the fourth moment of the vanishing mediator à la Zizek in Deleuze and Guattari.

If the first part of Anti-Oedipus constructs, at the level of the unconscious, a speculative identity of desiring-production with its own products, the third part re-inscribes that identity at the level of “universal history.” The three syntheses—production, recording, consumption—are now mapped onto three configurations of the socius, each of which functions as a “full body” on which the flows of desire are inscribed and bound. Already in Part I Deleuze and Guattari specify that “the social machine or socius may be the body of the Earth, the body of the Despot, the body of Money” and insist that “the prime function incumbent upon the socius has always been to codify the flows of desire” (Deleuze and Guattari 33). The triad Earth/Despot/Money anticipates, in condensed form, the later sequence “savages, barbarians, civilized men”: the territorial socius of the earth, the despotic socius of the emperor, and the capitalist socius of money. What appears in Part III under the guise of an anthropological stadial narrative is thus already implicit in the speculative identity of desiring- production and social production that Part I establishes.

The shift from the unconscious to universal history is not a passage from “micro” to “macro” but a variation in régime within the same process. Deleuze and Guattari repeatedly deny any difference in nature between psychic and social production: “desiring-production is first and foremost social in nature, and tends to free itself only at the end,” and “the prime function incumbent upon the socius” is to inscribe and regulate flows which are, at the same time, libidinal and economic (33). The socius is not an external container that would receive pre-constituted desires; it is the mode in which desiring- production is organized, coded, and remembered. The same speculative identity that, in Part I, linked desiring-machines to their products now links social machines to desiring-production: the socius is both the product of desiring-production and that which conditions the latter’s reproduction. Universal history, in this sense, is nothing other than the history of the changing forms of this identity.

The territorial machine is introduced in “Savages, Barbarians, Civilized Men” as the “first form of socius, the machine of primitive inscription, the ‘megamachine’ that covers a social field” (141). The earth appears as the “great unengendered stasis” and the “surface on which the whole process of production is inscribed,” the quasi cause and object of desire itself (141). Coding is here literal: the socius marks bodies and the earth with scars, tattoos, totems, and territorial partitions. Deleuze and Guattari explicitly invoke Nietzsche’s Genealogy of Morals to characterize this as a “system of cruelty, a terrible alphabet”: “Man could never do without blood, torture, and sacrifices when he felt the need to create a memory for himself” (145). Cruelty is not a natural violence but “the movement of culture that is realized in bodies and inscribed on them,” the labor of a collective mnemotechnique that renders obligations unforgettable (145).

The territorial socius thus corresponds to what might be called an immediate being of the social. The codes that bind desire and production—kinship rules, rituals, distributions of land and goods—are not yet reflexively thematized as “law” or “representation.” They are coextensive with the earth-body itself, which Deleuze and Guattari describe as the locus where “desire becomes bound to its own repression” (141). The repression at stake here is not yet Oedipal or even familial; it is the binding of flows to marked places and marked bodies, the forging of a collective memory through the cruelty of inscription. From the standpoint of Hegel’s Logic , this regime stands closest to the standpoint of “abstract identity” in which being is taken as immediately given and self-identical; contradiction and mediation remain latent within it.

The despotic machine appears when this immediate identity of earth and code is broken. Deleuze and Guattari describe the despotic State, “such as it appears in the purest conditions of ‘Asiatic’ production,” as having two “correlative aspects”: “on the one hand it replaces the territorial machine, it forms a new deterritorialized full body; on the other hand it maintains the old territorialities, integrates them as parts or organs of production in the new machine” (198). The earth is no longer the socius; it has been supplanted by the body of the despot, the locus of a transcendent signifier that overcodes all prior inscriptions. Codes that were once immanent to the earth become subordinate to the law emanating from the sovereign. Writing, in turn, is no longer the dispersed marking of bodies but an “overwriting” that redistributes obligations, debts, and alliances in reference to the despot.

If the territorial socius is the regime in which coding remains embedded in the earth-body, the despotic socius introduces a dimension of abstract negativity. The despot’s body functions as a point of exteriority vis-à-vis the social field. Overcoding negates the autonomy of local codes, subordinating them to a centralized apparatus of capture. Deleuze and Guattari repeatedly stress that the “signifier” in its Lacanian sense is historically tied to this despotic configuration: the signifier is the “imperial” element that welds desire to the law and installs a vertical hierarchy over previously horizontal codes (209). The despotic machine is thus the social realization of a first negation: it is not simply the external opposite of the territorial machine, but the latter’s own “inner other,” the form into which the territorial configuration passes when its presuppositions (surplus, stock, sedentary administration) are explicated.

At the same time, the despotic machine does not abolish the territorial codes; it preserves and rearranges them. Territorialities persist as parts or “organs” of the new machine; cruel inscriptions and local obligations are retained, but subordinated to imperial tribute. In Hegelian terms, the despotic socius corresponds to contradiction: the identity of the social field is no longer immediate but split between the transcendence of the despot and the immanence of the earth, between the unity of law and the multiplicity of local practices. The despot’s body is not merely another object among others; it is the site where the social field reflects into itself as law. The speculative identity of desiring- production and socius is here mediated by a signifier that functions as negativity.

The capitalist socius, by contrast, is characterized not by coding or overcoding, but by an axiomatic that operates on decoded flows. Deleuze and Guattari insist that capitalism “engineers and mobilizes flows that are effectively decoded,” but does so “by substituting for the codes a quantifying axiomatic that is even more oppressive” (182–83). Capitalism breaks the traditional codes of kinship, land, and status; it deterritorializes labor, money, and the body itself. Yet this deterritorialization is never absolute. It is immediately reinscribed in an axiomatic that sets “conversion rates” between flows—wages, prices, interest, profit—so as to render them commensurable within the circuit of capital. The “full body” is no longer the earth or the despot but money, or more precisely, capital-as-value-in-process.

Here the speculative identity of production and its conditions takes a new form. Capitalism is defined by an interminable process of self-overcoming: crises continually force it to “negate itself from within,” transforming its own axioms in order to preserve its global reproduction. The negativity at work in capitalism is thus self-relating. The system survives only by continually negating its own determinate forms—breaking monopolies, deregulating markets, re-territorializing populations, inventing new financial instruments. Deleuze and Guattari describe capitalism as a “relative limit” that constantly approaches but never coincides with its own “absolute” limit: the point at which decoded flows completely escape the axiomatic (182–84).

From a Hegelian perspective, this regime bears the closest resemblance to “concrete universality.” Capitalism posits its own abstract measure—value—as the universal equivalent of all commodities, while at the same time generating a concrete network of institutions, laws, and practices that sustain this equivalence. The universality of money is inseparable from the particular forms of wage labor, property, and contract that realize it. Moreover, the system incorporates its own negativity as an internal motor: crises, contradictions, and breakdowns are not simply external threats but the very mechanisms through which capital renovates itself. The speculative identity that was, in Part I, articulated as the identity of production and product, and, in Hegel, as the identity of identity and non-identity, now appears in historical guise as the identity of capital with its own negations.

It is at this point that Deleuze and Guattari introduce a distinction between the relative limit (capitalism) and the absolute limit (the body without organs as deterritorialized socius). “We shall speak of an absolute limit,” they write, “every time the schizo-flows pass through the wall, scramble all the codes, and deterritorialize the socius: the body without organs is the deterritorialized socius, the wilderness where the decoded flows run free, the end of the world, the apocalypse” (181). By contrast, the capitalist social formation is the relative limit because it mobilizes decoded flows but “substitutes for the codes a quantifying axiomatic,” thereby keeping the flows compatible with the reproduction of capital (182–83). Absolute deterritorialization is thus simultaneously produced and blocked; it appears as a virtual horizon that capitalism must approach in order to function but cannot cross without self- dissolution.

The schizophrenic process is the name Deleuze and Guattari give to this absolute limit as it is experienced from within capitalism. Schizophrenia is not an outside or a pre-capitalist remainder; it is the point where the decoding of flows reaches such intensity that the axiomatic cannot keep pace.

“Capitalism and schizophrenia” are not two separate domains but a single field in which deterritorialization is alternately captured and released. The “new earth” and the “new people” evoked at the end of Part III designate not a positive fourth social formation but the immanent limit of universal history itself—the point where the speculative identity of desiring-production and socius ceases to be mediated by a specific full body (earth, despot, money) and appears as pure process (Deleuze and Guattari 192–99).

If one now juxtaposes the universal history of Anti-Oedipus with the speculative identities traced in the first section, the homology with Hegel’s dialectic becomes difficult to ignore. Territorial, despotic, and capitalist socii correspond, respectively, to an immediate social being, a mediated negativity, and a self- relating universality. Each new configuration does not simply replace the previous one; it retroactively reinterprets and reorders it. The despotic machine preserves territorial codes as subordinate organs; the capitalist machine preserves both territorial and despotic elements—land, state, law—as components of its axiomatic. Similarly, in Hegel’s Logic , each new category re-inscribes the previous ones as moments within a larger whole. Deleuze and Guattari’s explicit rejection of dialectics does not prevent their universal history from reproducing its structural logic.

At the same time, the insistence on the limit—the body without organs as deterritorialized socius, the schizo-flows that scramble all codes—introduces an element that does not sit comfortably within a triadic schema. The absolute limit is neither a new social machine nor a transcendent outside; it is an excessive dimension internal to every socius, the point where desiring-production escapes the codifications that sustain social reproduction. This excess is most visible in capitalism because capitalism brings decoding to its highest pitch while remaining structurally incapable of sustaining an absolute deterritorialization. In this sense, universal history in Anti-Oedipus is organized not only by a triad (territorial, despotic, capitalist) but also by an uncounted fourth term: the limit-process that traverses and exceeds all three.

The second section thus arrives at a paradoxical conclusion. Deleuze and Guattari’s universal history is formally dialectical: it passes from an immediate social being through a negating overcoding to a regime of self-relating negativity in which universality is concretely realized. Yet this history also depends on an internal excess—the absolute limit of deterritorialization—that cannot be accommodated within a simple triad. The next movement of the essay will take up Slavoj Žižek’s reconstruction of Hegel’s dialectic as a four-term process governed by a “vanishing mediator,” in order to show how the schizophrenic limit of Anti-Oedipus can be read as a Deleuzian counterpart to this surplus term: the uncounted “fourth” that both propels and destabilizes the universal history of the socius.

Structural Quadruplicity If the preceding sections reconstructed Anti-Oedipus as staging, successively, a speculative ontology of desiring-production, a phenomenology of Oedipal subjectivation, and a universal history of the socius, the next step is to register how this tripartite movement is shadowed by a fourth moment that does not quite fit—schizoanalysis as analytic praxis. The book’s division into four parts is not a merely editorial convenience. It encodes, at the level of composition, something like the very quadruplicity that Slavoj Žižek extracts from Hegel via the Greimasian semiotic square: an excessive “fourth” that both completes and destabilizes the dialectical triad.

On the face of it, Anti-Oedipus is rigorously anti-dialectical. Its first part installs desiring-production as positive, immanent synthesis; the second exposes Oedipus and lack as reactive paralogisms; the third unfolds a universal history of social machines—territorial, despotic, capitalist; the fourth proposes schizoanalysis as an anti-psychoanalytic practice. Yet the text is punctuated by structural figures that already speak in the language of “three plus something.” A particularly telling passage is the well-known remark about the impossibility of folding primitive social relations into the Oedipal triad. When Deleuze and Guattari gloss Kardiner on the “universal Oedipal limit,” they insist that in so-called primitive formations “no reduction whatever … is possible that would answer to the formula 3+1 (the four corners of the field folded into three, like a tablecloth, plus the transcendent term that performs the folding operation)” (Deleuze and Guattari 177). What is at stake, they suggest, is not an absent Oedipus, but another arithmetic altogether: “At the heart of primitive production one always finds oneself at 4+n, in the system of ancestors and affines. Far from being able to claim that here there is no end to Oedipus, one sees that it never manages to begin; one is always brought to a halt well before 3+1” (177–78).

Already in this local polemic against the 3+1 of Oedipus, a different schema emerges. The problem is not simply that Oedipus is a “bad” transcendent term that folds a four-cornered social field into a familial triangle; the problem is that the very form 3+1 is the wrong combinatory. The primitive socius, Deleuze and Guattari insist, is configured as 4+n, a kind of proliferating quadruplicity that refuses both the reduction to three and the transcendence of a single “one” that would suture the series. That small notational twist—3+1 versus 4+n—already situates Anti-Oedipus in proximity to the semiotic square that Žižek mobilizes in “Why Should a Dialectician Learn to Count to Four?” as the formalization of the “surplus” inherent to dialectical movement.

Žižek’s point of departure is the standard Hegelian triad of immediacy, its negation, and the “negation of negation,” understood as the restoration of a mediated immediacy. But as he carefully re-reads Hegel’s Logic , this triad proves structurally incomplete. The first immediacy is transformed into its own opposite; the second moment, negativity, is not simply external opposition but the “inner negativity” of the first; the third moment, self-relating negativity, appears as the reconciliation of the previous two.

What gets lost in the abstract triad, Žižek argues, is the status of the mediator itself—the “pure difference” that “counts for nothing,” the vanishing moment without which the passage from immediacy to mediated universality could not occur (Žižek, “Why Should a Dialectician” 3–4). This is where his appropriation of the Greimasian semiotic square becomes decisive. Starting from a simple opposition (A / non-A), Greimas introduces the additional positions “neither A nor non-A” and “both A and non-A.” The effect is to exhibit, in formal terms, the supplementary element that the triad had repressed: the site of structural impossibility that nonetheless conditions the entire field.

Žižek’s “counting to four” is thus not numerological inflation but the formal inscription of a constitutive excess—the dimension of what he calls “surplus-nothing,” that which “counts for nothing” and yet is the very motor of the dialectical process (3–4). If one juxtaposes this with Deleuze and Guattari’s insistence on 4+n against 3+1, the affinity becomes difficult to ignore. For the latter, it is precisely the attempt to force the social field into the formula 3+1—three familial terms plus the phallic transcendence—that produces Oedipus as an oppressive limit. Against this, they insist on a combinatoire in which four is not “three plus One” but a minimal multiplicity that is always already exceeded (“+n”). Holland’s gloss is instructive here: drawing on Greimas, he notes that Deleuze and Guattari’s use of “semantic combinatoire” signals an attentiveness to how the addition of a new term to a dualism or triad “redistributes the entire field of possible relations,” and that they themselves gesture toward a “fourth socius,” a “new earth” that would follow capital (Holland 136).

At the macro-level of the book’s architecture, this combinatorial logic is repeated. Part I (“The Desiring-Machines”) presents desiring-production as pure positivity, the immanent synthesis of flows and partial objects. Part II (“Psychoanalysis and Familialism”) stages the becoming-negative of desire in the form of Oedipal repression and the paralogisms of psychoanalysis. Part III (“Savages, Barbarians, Civilized Men”) redescribes both desiring-production and Oedipalization at the level of the socius, in effect re-articulating them as moments of a universal history. Part IV (“Introduction to Schizoanalysis”) withdraws from both the ontological and historical planes to articulate an analytic practice that intervenes in the very configurations that Parts I–III have constructed. Read through Žižek’s Hegelian tetrad, Part I corresponds to immediacy (desire as productive positivity), Part II to its inner negativity (Oedipalization and lack), Part III to the negation of negation (universal history as the self-relating negativity of the socius), and Part IV to the vanishing mediator—the schizoanalytic act that is both generated by and retroactively reconfigures the prior three.

This is not to claim that Deleuze and Guattari “are really Hegelian,” as if the entire project of

Capitalism and Schizophrenia could be seamlessly reintegrated into speculative logic. It is, however, to

suggest that the book’s four-part structure functions as the analogue of Žižek’s semiotic square: schizoanalysis marks the structural place of the “supplementary” fourth term, the locus of intervention that cannot be straightforwardly incorporated into the ontology of desiring-machines, the genealogy of Oedipus, or the universal history of the socius, even though all three presuppose it. Schizoanalysis is neither another theory of desire, nor another figure of the socius; it is the praxis that retroactively determines what counts as desiring-production and what counts as a “social machine” in the first place.

In Žižek’s vocabulary, it occupies the position of the subject as vanishing mediator: the act that, once accomplished, erases its own traces and appears as if the preceding configuration had always already been there.

Judith Butler’s critical engagement with Deleuze in Subjects of Desire complicates this picture in a productive way. For Butler, Deleuze’s philosophy of desire represents “the culmination of a certain French anti-Hegelianism,” one that seeks to displace negativity (lack, contradiction, recognition) with “productive force” and “difference” (Butler 205). Yet she argues that this anti-Hegelian gesture remains structurally entangled with the dialectic it seeks to overcome. Deleuze can only articulate desire as productive positivity against the background of a Hegelian problematic in which desire had been defined as lack and recognition; the very notion of a “repetition of difference,” Butler suggests, presupposes a prior history of negativity from which it seeks to liberate itself (205–07). In the chapter tellingly titled “From Slave Morality to Productive Desire,” she shows how Deleuze’s reading of Nietzsche reconfigures ressentiment and slave morality into a machine for producing active forces; yet this transformation still relies on a logic of sublation, a passage from reactive to active that is formally indistinguishable from dialectical overcoming (Butler 209–12).

From Butler’s vantage point, Deleuze is caught in a paradox. On the one hand, he refuses Hegel’s “labor of the negative,” treating negation as derivative, reactive, a mere effect of the interruption of flows. On the other, his own account of desire cannot avoid staging events of break, reversal, and reconfiguration that function precisely like negations. The move from Oedipalized desire to desiring- production, or from fascist micro-investments to revolutionary ones, is not a simple intensification of positivity; it is a kind of passage through negativity that the text strives to disavow. Butler’s critique thus converges, from another angle, on the necessity of a “fourth” term: a place in which the very operation that transforms reactive into active is acknowledged as an act, rather than subsumed under the anonymous productivity of desire. In other words, what Deleuze attributes to impersonal life or “body without organs” may well be, in Hegelian terms, the work of the subject that his conceptual apparatus has no place for.

Žižek’s Organs without Bodies radicalizes this line of questioning by explicitly re-routing Deleuze through Hegel and Lacan. The book opens by privileging a “tension” within Deleuze’s corpus: between the “vitalist” Deleuze of Difference and Repetition and Anti-Oedipus , who affirms a pre-personal plane of immanence, and the more austere Deleuze of The Logic of Sense , who thematizes the impersonal event and the sterility of sense. Rather than choosing between them, Žižek proposes to read Deleuze as a thinker of drive in the Lacanian sense—that is, of a repetitive, excessive movement that has no positive object and that is structurally closer to Hegelian negativity than to any naïve vitalism (Žižek, Organs without Bodies 1–4). The “organs without bodies” of the title thus name the dimension of pure difference that cannot be gathered into any harmonious “body of life,” and that resonates uncannily From this angle, the fourfold structure of Anti-Oedipus is not an innocent narrative convenience but the symptom of an unresolved tension in Deleuze and Guattari’s project. Parts I–III can be read as the triadic exposition of a materialist ontology and history; Part IV, in turn, functions as the supplementary square that indexes what the triad cannot absorb. Schizoanalysis, as the praxis that intervenes in desiring-production, Oedipus, and the socius, performs the role of the vanishing mediator that Žižek attributes to Protestantism in his Hegelian re-reading of Weber: it is both the condition of possibility for a new configuration (a non-Oedipal investment of desire, a non-State form of the socius) and the element that must efface itself for that configuration to appear as “natural.” In Butler’s terms, this is precisely where the subject returns in the guise of its own erasure: as the anonymous “machine” that, in actu, is nothing other than a decision, a cut, a re-articulation of relations.

To say that a “Deleuzian must learn to count to four” is therefore to insist that Anti-Oedipus cannot be adequately grasped within the terms of its own avowed anti-dialectics. Once the quadruplicity of its structure is read alongside Žižek’s semiotic square, Butler’s critique of productive desire, and Žižek’s Hegelianization of Deleuze in Organs without Bodies , the book appears as a text that both presupposes and disavows the place of a subject—understood not as a substantial ego but as the locus of a vanishing act. The “fourth” is nothing mysterious: it is simply the name for that dimension of negativity, decision, or intervention that Deleuze and Guattari must mobilize whenever they speak of “breaking with Oedipus,” “deterritorializing capital,” or “creating a new earth,” but which their ontology of positive production cannot explicitly accommodate. Counting to four thus means, for a Deleuzian, not the abandonment of immanence, but the acknowledgment that immanence itself is structured by an internal excess that looks, from a certain angle, uncannily like Hegel.

After the Triad If the previous sections reconstructed Anti-Oedipus as secretly organized by a Hegelian threefold—

speculative identity in Part I, the becoming-negative of desire in Part II, concrete universality of the socius in Part III—plus a fourth, schizoanalytic moment in Part IV, the remaining task is to ask what follows from this once one shifts from universal history to the present and beyond Anti-Oedipus itself.

At the level of explicit doctrine, Anti-Oedipus is strikingly cautious about communism. Capitalism is said to be “the limit of all societies,” the formation in which desiring-production finally appears as such, but only under the regime of an axiomatic that perpetually converts decoded flows into the reproduction of capital (Deleuze and Guattari, Anti-Oedipus 153–54). The “absolute limit” is named as the body without organs as deterritorialized socius, the point at which “schizo-flows pass through the wall, scramble all the codes, and deterritorialize the socius: the body without organs is the deterritorialized socius, the wilderness where the decoded flows run free, the end of the world, the apocalypse” (181).

This limit is never directly occupied; it is approached, warded off, and displaced. The relative limit of capitalism is precisely the system’s capacity to make this absolute limit function as its own motor without ever allowing it to actualize itself.

Within this frame, communism cannot appear as a fourth “mode of production” in the Marxist sense.

Every attempt to posit it as a “new social machine” would immediately reintroduce the logic of the socius: coding, overcoding, or axiomatization on a new full body. Instead, Anti-Oedipus speaks of a “new earth” and a “new people,” of revolutionary machines and lines of flight, of the proletariat as the class that “is all the more determined as it is not yet a class” (Deleuze and Guattari 153, 341–42).

Communism is displaced into the position of the absolute limit: not a stage after capitalism, but the internal edge at which the capitalist axiomatic threatens to collapse. In the language of the semiotic square, it occupies the position that is neither the simple affirmation of capital’s deterritorializing flows, nor their reactionary negation, but the point at which the very opposition between “inside” and “outside” the system becomes undecidable.

Counting to four here means recognizing that this “fourth” is not an optional supplement but structurally required. The territorial, despotic, and capitalist socii can only be narrated as a coherent universal history if the process that exceeds them all—the process of deterritorialization that they variously bind, overcode, and axiomatize—is presupposed. The clandestine subject of universal history is neither Spirit nor capital, but this anonymous process itself. Yet Anti-Oedipus also shows that this process is not, in itself, politically determinate. Lines of flight can culminate in catatonia, suicide, or fascism as easily as in revolution; absolute deterritorialization can be indistinguishable from implosion.

The fourth term is thus structurally necessary and normatively ambiguous.

A Thousand Plateaus radicalizes this ambiguity and, in doing so, makes the tetradic structure more

explicit. The universal history of the three socii recedes; in its place appears a “geology of morals” that operates not with “stages” but with strata, abstract machines, assemblages, and planes of consistency.

The triads do not disappear but proliferate. There are strata of content and expression; there are apparatuses of capture that mirror the despotic State; there are “war machines” that function as exteriorities to the State; there are smooth and striated spaces, major and minor languages. But the systematic opposition is always doubled by a fourth element: the plane of consistency on which all articulations ultimately “melt into air,” the “Body without Organs” now understood less as a psychoanalytic figure than as the limit-plane on which intensities circulate without being organized into stable forms (Deleuze and Guattari, A Thousand Plateaus 40–45).

In this second volume, the fourth term is no longer hidden in the architecture of the book; it is thematized as the plane of consistency which is “neither an undifferentiated continuity, nor an unconstituted chaos, but a plan(e) of immanence” (ATP 266–67). Every assemblage is double- articulated—strata and destratification, territorialization and deterritorialization—but the plane of consistency is what allows assemblages to form and transform at all. It occupies the same structural place as the body without organs and the absolute limit in Anti-Oedipus , yet it is now explicitly registered as the site of a constructive practice: one “makes a Body without Organs” by carefully undoing particular strata, not by leaping into an amorphous nothing (ATP 150–54). The learning to count to four is codified here as a rule of conduct: one must hold together strata (the first three “terms”) and the plane of consistency (the fourth) without confusing them.

Viewed from this angle, the figure of communism implicit in A Thousand Plateaus is no longer that of a “fourth socius” in the old universal-historical sense, but the practical problem of how to compose a plane of consistency that does not immediately collapse back into the State or into death. The war machine is not a ready-made revolutionary subject; it is an abstract machine that can be captured by the State (as in fascism) or become the vehicle for new forms of collective life. Absolute deterritorialization is no longer utopian in itself; it has become structurally indistinguishable from the control mechanisms that, as Deleuze later remarks, replace disciplinary enclosures with continuous modulation, “a corporation replacing the factory, perpetual training replacing the school, continuous control replacing the examination” (Deleuze, “Postscript on Control Societies” 4–5). In other words, once the fourth term is acknowledged, it becomes clear that capitalism itself lives at the “edge of chaos,” using the very deterritorializing processes that Anti-Oedipus had treated as potentially revolutionary as levers of its own reproduction.

This forces a shift in how the “labour of the negative” is understood. In Anti-Oedipus , negativity was largely displaced onto antiproduction and Oedipus: repressive apparatuses that interrupt the flows of desire. But once capitalism is seen as incorporating negativity into its own axiomatic—internalizing crisis, breakdown, and “creative destruction” as modes of self-reproduction—the mere affirmation of deterritorialization is no longer sufficient. The fourth term cannot simply be the celebration of lines of flight; it must be the name for a determinate reconfiguration of the relations that define the socius.

This is why the later Deleuze insists that a politics adequate to control societies cannot simply oppose control to freedom, but must invent “new ways of being” and “new kinds of rights” immanent to the very technologies of control (Deleuze, “Postscript” 7–8). The negativity at stake here is not the old dialectical negation of negation, but a cutting, a selection, a local refusal that alters the topology of the assemblage.

From the standpoint of the Hegelian dialectic reconstructed earlier, this corresponds to the insistence that the mediator—the fourth term that “counts for nothing” in the triad—must be recognized as an act. In Anti-Oedipus , schizoanalysis is introduced as a practice that “refuses interpretation,” that “never asks to what a given desire is a lack,” but asks instead “how it works, what it produces” (Deleuze and Guattari, Anti-Oedipus 109–10). The analyst is supposed to operate not by decoding symbols, but by mapping connections and disjunctions, by “plugging in” desiring-machines otherwise than Oedipally.

Yet this practice, in order to be anything more than a descriptive cartography, must eventually decide: to break with certain investments, to encourage others, to construct new assemblages. The place where it decides is precisely the place that the Hegelian dialectic had named as that of the subject—the vanishing mediator that acts, and then lets the result appear as if it had always already been necessary.

Bringing the two volumes together, one can say that Anti-Oedipus constructs the field in which the fourth term becomes thinkable while A Thousand Plateaus gives the technical vocabulary for working in that field without collapsing into either a naïve vitalism or a melancholic dialectics. To “learn to count to four” as a Deleuzian is, then, to accept three constraints at once. First, that there is no outside of the socius: desire is always already social, and any communism worth the name must be immanent to the transformation of the existing relations of production, not their negation from a transcendent elsewhere. Second, that there is no simple triad of stages culminating in capitalism or its abolition: universal history is always knotted to a fourth term, the limit-process that can just as well be captured by capital as turned against it. Third, that any politics adequate to this situation must assume the form of an immanent construction: the composition of new planes of consistency, new war machines, new “peoples who are missing,” rather than the restoration of a lost community or the fulfillment of a pre- given telos.

In that sense, the question “why must a Deleuzian learn to count to four?” is neither an accusation nor a mere joke at the expense of anti-Hegelianism. It names a structural necessity that Anti-Oedipus itself half acknowledges and half disavows. The first three parts of the book—desiring-machines, psychoanalysis and familialism, savages–barbarians–civilized men—can be read as the exposition of a triadic logic that remains formally Hegelian. The fourth part, “Introduction to Schizoanalysis,” is where the act that would transform that logic is supposed to take place, but its status remains hazy, oscillating between a neutral “cartography” and a quasi-messianic promise of a new earth. Once the tetradic structure is made explicit, A Thousand Plateaus and the later texts on control pick up exactly here by forcing the fourth term to appear as a problem of practice, a problem of how to intervene in the very processes that had previously been simply “affirmed.” Universal history after Deleuze is therefore neither the story of Spirit coming to know itself nor the sober recognition that “there is no alternative” to the capitalist axiomatic. It is the unstable configuration in which the labour of the negative and the production of desire are recognized as coextensive, and in which the fourth term—the vanishing mediator, the line of flight, the plane of consistency, the communism that is not-yet—can no longer be left implicit. Counting to four is the minimal arithmetic of that recognition.
